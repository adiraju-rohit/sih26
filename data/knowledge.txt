TOPIC: What is a Cooperative Society
A cooperative society is a voluntary association of individuals who join together to achieve common economic, social, and cultural goals through a jointly owned and democratically controlled enterprise. Membership is open and voluntary, and each member usually has equal voting rights regardless of the amount of capital contributed, following the principle of "one member, one vote".

TOPIC: Registration of a Cooperative Society
A cooperative society is registered under the relevant state Cooperative Societies Act or, for societies operating in more than one state, under the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act. To register, a minimum number of persons (usually ten or more, or as specified by the Act) must submit an application to the Registrar of Cooperative Societies along with the proposed bye-laws, a list of members, and proof of the society's economic viability. On approval, the Registrar issues a certificate of registration, and the society becomes a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal.

TOPIC: Bye-laws of a Cooperative Society
Bye-laws are the internal rules of a cooperative society that govern its objectives, membership criteria, capital structure, management, meetings, distribution of profits, and dissolution procedure. Bye-laws must be consistent with the governing Cooperative Societies Act and are approved by the Registrar at the time of registration. Any amendment to the bye-laws requires approval of the general body followed by registration of the amendment with the Registrar.

TOPIC: Membership Rights and Obligations
Every member of a cooperative society has the right to participate in general meetings, vote on resolutions, stand for election to the managing committee, receive a share of the surplus (dividend or patronage bonus) as per the bye-laws, and access the society's records within the limits prescribed by law. Members are obligated to pay their share capital, abide by the bye-laws, attend meetings, and use the services of the society honestly. A member may be expelled for conduct prejudicial to the interests of the society after due process.

TOPIC: Managing Committee and Governance Structure
The managing committee (also called the board of directors) is elected by members in the general body meeting and is responsible for the overall management of the society, including implementation of policies, appointment of staff, and financial oversight. The committee typically has a chairperson, vice-chairperson, secretary, and treasurer. Elections to the managing committee are conducted periodically, generally every five years, under the supervision of the state Cooperative Election Authority or a similar body. Committee members owe a fiduciary duty to act in good faith and in the best interests of the society.

TOPIC: General Body Meetings
The general body, comprising all members, is the supreme authority of the cooperative society. An Annual General Meeting (AGM) must be held within a specified period after the close of the financial year to approve the annual accounts, elect or ratify committee members, approve the budget, and decide on distribution of profits. A Special General Meeting may be convened to address urgent matters. Quorum requirements and notice periods for meetings are specified in the bye-laws and the Act.

TOPIC: Audit and Accountability
Every cooperative society is required to have its accounts audited annually by a qualified auditor empanelled by the Registrar or by a chartered accountant, depending on the size and category of the society. The audit report examines the financial statements, verifies compliance with statutory provisions, and highlights irregularities if any. Societies must submit the audit report to the Registrar within a prescribed period and rectify any defects pointed out.

TOPIC: Share Capital and Reserve Fund
Members contribute share capital at the time of joining, which forms the ownership stake in the society. A statutory reserve fund is created every year by setting aside a percentage of net profits (commonly around twenty-five percent, as prescribed under the Act) to strengthen the financial stability of the society. Reserve funds generally cannot be distributed to members and are used to cover future losses or for approved cooperative development purposes.

TOPIC: Distribution of Profits and Patronage
After statutory deductions such as the reserve fund, education fund, and other prescribed appropriations, the remaining surplus may be distributed to members as dividend on share capital (subject to a ceiling prescribed by the Act) or as patronage bonus based on the volume of business each member conducted with the society. This ensures that benefits are linked to participation rather than purely to capital contribution.

TOPIC: Dispute Resolution and Arbitration
Disputes between members, or between a member and the society, relating to management, business, or elections are generally referred to arbitration under the Cooperative Societies Act rather than to ordinary civil courts. The Registrar or an authorized arbitrator hears such disputes and passes an award, which is enforceable in the same manner as a civil court decree. This mechanism is intended to provide a faster and more specialized forum for resolving cooperative disputes.

TOPIC: Winding Up and Dissolution
A cooperative society may be wound up voluntarily by a resolution of the general body or compulsorily by an order of the Registrar on grounds such as failure to commence business, persistent violation of the Act, or inability to pay debts. On winding up, a liquidator is appointed to realize the assets, discharge liabilities, and distribute any remaining surplus among members according to the bye-laws and the Act.

TOPIC: Types of Cooperative Societies
Cooperative societies operate across several sectors, including agricultural credit societies, primary agricultural cooperative societies (PACS), dairy cooperatives, housing cooperatives, consumer cooperatives, marketing cooperatives, and cooperative banks. Each type is governed by additional sector-specific regulations, for example dairy cooperatives may follow guidelines issued by the National Dairy Development Board, while cooperative banks are additionally regulated by the Reserve Bank of India for banking-related activities.

TOPIC: Role of the Registrar of Cooperative Societies
The Registrar is the statutory authority responsible for registration, supervision, and regulation of cooperative societies within a state. Powers of the Registrar include approving bye-law amendments, ordering inspection or inquiry into the affairs of a society, superseding a mismanaged managing committee and appointing an administrator, and adjudicating disputes referred for arbitration. The Registrar also maintains the official register of all cooperative societies.

TOPIC: Government Schemes Supporting Cooperatives
Various government schemes support the cooperative sector, including subsidized credit through primary agricultural credit societies, the Revival Package for Short Term Cooperative Credit Structure, the National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC) schemes for financing processing, storage, and marketing infrastructure, and support under the Ministry of Cooperation for computerisation of PACS to improve transparency and efficiency. Housing cooperatives may also access schemes under state housing boards for affordable housing finance.

TOPIC: Ministry of Cooperation and National Cooperation Policy
The Ministry of Cooperation was created to provide a separate administrative, legal, and policy framework for strengthening the cooperative movement, deepening cooperatives as a true people-based movement, and streamlining processes for ease of doing business for cooperatives. It works towards convergence of various schemes, computerisation of Primary Agricultural Credit Societies, and formulation of a National Cooperation Policy to guide the sector's growth over the coming decades.

TOPIC: Constitutional Framework for Cooperative Societies in India
Cooperative societies are legally recognized and safeguarded under the Constitution of India through the 97th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2011. The amendment inserted Article 19(1)(c), declaring the right to form cooperative societies a fundamental right. It also introduced Article 43B in the Directive Principles of State Policy, obligating the State to promote voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control, and professional management of cooperative societies. Furthermore, Part IXB was added to define board composition, reservation for women and SC/ST candidates, mandatory audit timelines, and election guidelines. In July 2021, the Supreme Court of India upheld Part IXB in relation to Multi-State Cooperative Societies (Union List, Entry 44), while clarifying that cooperatives operating within a single state remain under the legislative competence of State Legislatures (State List, Entry 32).

TOPIC: Multi-State Cooperative Societies (MSCS) Act 2002 and 2023 Amendments
The Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act, 2002 governs cooperative bodies whose objectives and operational jurisdictions span across more than one state or union territory. To eliminate loopholes, enhance financial discipline, and restore democratic accountability, Parliament enacted the Multi-State Cooperative Societies (Amendment) Act, 2023. Key reforms introduced under the 2023 Amendment include the establishment of an independent Cooperative Election Authority, mandatory reservation for one SC/ST and two women members on boards, the appointment of a Cooperative Ombudsman for member grievance redressal, creation of a Cooperative Rehabilitation and Development Fund, and severe penalties for electoral and operational malpractices.

TOPIC: Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS)
The Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS) is appointed under Section 4 of the MSCS Act by the Central Government. The CRCS possesses statutory powers to register multi-state cooperatives, approve or reject amendments to bye-laws, conduct administrative inquiries, inspect accounting books, summon officers, order audits, supersede failing boards, and appoint liquidators. Under the digital transformation initiative of the Ministry of Cooperation, a comprehensive CRCS portal was launched to process applications, amendments, annual returns, and audit filings online with complete transparency and time-bound statutory workflows.

TOPIC: Model Bye-Laws for Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS)
Model Bye-Laws formulated by the Ministry of Cooperation in consultation with States empower Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) to transform from mere credit disbursement entities into multidimensional economic hubs. Under the Model Bye-Laws, PACS are authorized to engage in over 25 diversified business activities. These include operating fair price shops, common service centres (CSCs), retail petrol and diesel dispensing outlets, LPG distributorships, customized hiring centres for agricultural implements, cold storage and warehousing, rural pipe water supply maintenance, and procurement of milk and seeds. States have widely adopted these Model Bye-Laws to enhance rural employment and financial self-sustainability.

TOPIC: Computerization of Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS)
The Centrally Sponsored Scheme for the Computerization of PACS is a flagship national initiative by the Ministry of Cooperation aimed at modernizing 63,000 functional PACS nationwide. The project deploys a cloud-based unified Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) software designed by NABARD. This ERP software standardizes accounting, integrates PACS with District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) and State Cooperative Banks (StCBs), supports regional languages, and automates credit disbursement, storage tracking, and subsidy distribution. This digital overhaul prevents manual manipulation of records, increases transparency, and guarantees real-time financial auditing.

TOPIC: PACS as Common Service Centres (CSCs)
Through a strategic partnership between the Ministry of Cooperation and the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY), PACS across India have been onboarded as Common Service Centres (CSCs). This enables rural cooperative societies to deliver more than 300 government-to-citizen (G2C) and business-to-consumer (B2C) digital services. Key services provided at village-level PACS include banking correspondent services, Aadhaar registration and updates, PAN card generation, utility bill payments, insurance enrollment, bus and train ticket bookings, and access to welfare benefits under schemes like PM-KISAN and Ayushman Bharat.

TOPIC: World's Largest Grain Storage Plan in the Cooperative Sector
The Government of India approved the World's Largest Grain Storage Plan in the Cooperative Sector, integrating schemes across Agriculture, Food Processing, and Food & Public Distribution. This plan creates decentralized storage capacity at the PACS level by establishing godowns with capacities ranging from 500 MT to 2,000 MT, along with custom hiring centres, grain dryers, sorting and grading units, and cold chains. By storing produce at the village level, farmers avoid distress sales, reduce transportation and post-harvest losses, and can avail post-harvest pledge finance using electronic Negotiable Warehouse Receipts (e-NWRs) issued through the WDRA framework.

TOPIC: New Apex Multi-State Cooperatives: NCEL, NCOL, and BBSSL
To strengthen the global and domestic cooperative footprint, the Ministry of Cooperation established three specialized national-level multi-state cooperative societies:
1. National Cooperative Exports Limited (NCEL): Dedicated to aggregating surplus rural agricultural produce and dairy goods for international export, ensuring direct profits flow back to farmers.
2. National Cooperative Organics Limited (NCOL): Provides farm-to-table organic certification, branding, testing, and marketing under the umbrella brand 'Bharat Organics'.
3. Bharatiya Beej Sahakari Samiti Limited (BBSSL): Focuses on production, testing, certification, and distribution of high-yield, climate-resilient, and certified seeds through primary cooperatives.

TOPIC: Cooperative Election Authority (CEA)
Under Section 45 of the amended MSCS Act 2023, the Central Government established the Cooperative Election Authority to conduct free, fair, and timely elections for the boards of multi-state cooperative societies. The Authority is headed by a Chairperson and eligible members with administrative or judicial experience. The CEA standardizes voter list preparation, oversees polling schedules, resolves pre-poll disputes, and eliminates electoral tampering by incumbent managing committees. Cooperative boards are statutorily barred from conducting their own elections.

TOPIC: Cooperative Ombudsman and Redressal Mechanism
The MSCS (Amendment) Act 2023 introduced Section 85A to establish one or more Cooperative Ombudsmen appointed by the Central Government. The Ombudsman handles complaints from members concerning non-admission of eligible applicants, withholding of dividends or passbooks, delay in transfer of shares, failure to convene general meetings, or irregularities in services. The Ombudsman conducts independent inquiries and is mandated to issue binding awards within ninety days. In addition, cooperative societies are required to designate a Cooperative Information Officer to furnish lawful records to members within 30 days.

TOPIC: Board Composition and Mandatory Inclusivity in Cooperatives
Under the MSCS Act 2023, the board of a multi-state cooperative society can have a maximum of twenty-one directors. To guarantee social inclusion, the Act statutorily mandates reservation of two seats for women and one seat for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes. Furthermore, co-opted directors possessing specialized expertise in banking, finance, risk management, or agricultural technology may be inducted (up to two members), but they do not hold voting rights in elections of office-bearers or contribute to quorum requirements.

TOPIC: Disqualification of Board Members and Office-Bearers
A member is disqualified from being chosen as or remaining a director if they: (a) have been convicted of an offence involving moral turpitude or economic offense, (b) are an undischarged insolvent, (c) default on loans taken from the society or have loan overdues exceeding statutory limits, (d) have failed to attend three consecutive board meetings without leave of absence, or (e) failed to convene the Annual General Meeting or complete statutory audit within the mandated timeframe. Disqualified directors may also face debarment from contesting cooperative elections for up to three consecutive years.

TOPIC: Financial Discipline, Reserve Fund, and Surplus Distribution
Every cooperative society is statutorily bound to allocate at least twenty-five percent of its net profit to the statutory Reserve Fund. Additionally, societies must contribute one percent to the Cooperative Education Fund managed by the National Cooperative Union of India (NCUI). Societies may also allocate funds toward building funds, dividend equalization funds, or community welfare funds as permitted by bye-laws. Dividends paid to members on their paid-up share capital cannot exceed the ceiling rate fixed under the governing Act (often capped between 12% and 15%), ensuring that cooperatives remain service-oriented rather than speculative profit vehicles.

TOPIC: Cooperative Rehabilitation and Development Fund (CRDF)
The 2023 Amendment to the MSCS Act established the Cooperative Rehabilitation and Development Fund (CRDF) under Section 70A. Multi-state cooperative societies that have generated net profits over the past three consecutive financial years are required to contribute one percent of their net profit to this fund. The proceeds are utilized to revive and financially restructure sick multi-state cooperative societies, safeguarding the savings of vulnerable depositors and maintaining systemic trust across the cooperative sector.

TOPIC: Statutory Audit and Concurrent Audit Obligations
Every multi-state cooperative society must appoint a qualified chartered accountant from a panel approved by the Central Registrar to audit its accounts annually. The statutory audit must be completed and submitted within six months of the close of the financial year. For societies having an annual turnover or deposit base exceeding prescribed thresholds (such as Rs 500 crore), concurrent audit or special government audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) or CRCS-empanelled auditors is mandated to prevent embezzlement and financial fraud.

TOPIC: Dispute Resolution and Arbitration under Section 84
Section 84 of the MSCS Act provides that any dispute touching the constitution, management, elections, or business of a multi-state cooperative society shall be referred to arbitration. Disputes can arise between members, past members, office-bearers, the board, or liquidators. Civil courts are expressly barred from entertaining suits where the statutory arbitrator has jurisdiction. The arbitration proceedings follow the principles of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and the award passed by the arbitrator carries the status and enforceability of a decree of a Civil Court.

TOPIC: National Cooperative Database (NCD)
The Ministry of Cooperation developed the comprehensive National Cooperative Database (NCD) to address the historical lack of centralized data. The database maps over 800,000 cooperative societies across all tiers (primary, district, state, and national levels) and diverse sectors such as agriculture, dairy, fisheries, weaving, consumer retail, and housing. The NCD provides policymakers, administrators, and investors with geo-tagged intelligence, operational health metrics, gap analyses for unserved panchayats, and actionable insights to drive targeted interventions.

TOPIC: Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) and RBI Regulatory Framework
Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) are registered under either the state Cooperative Societies Act or the MSCS Act for administrative purposes, but their banking operations are regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Following the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2020, the RBI holds expanded oversight over UCBs, including powers regarding board constitution, CEO qualification approvals, audit standards, capital adequacy (CRAR), loan concentration limits, resolution of stressed assets, and liquidation. The RBI classifies UCBs into a four-tiered regulatory hierarchy based on deposit size to implement proportionate prudential regulations.

TOPIC: Credit Delivery and Kisan Credit Card (KCC) via PACS
Primary Agricultural Credit Societies serve as the grassroots link for delivering short-term crop loans and working capital credit to small and marginal farmers. Through the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) scheme, PACS provide timely, collateral-free crop loans at concessional interest rates under the Modified Interest Subvention Scheme (MISS). Farmers repaying on or before the due date receive prompt repayment incentive subvention, effectively reducing the borrowing rate to 4% per annum. PACS also provide term credit for allied agricultural activities like dairy farming, goat rearing, and aquaculture.

TOPIC: Dairy Cooperatives and NDDB Convergence
India's dairy sector relies on the cooperative structure popularized by the 'Amul' model, organized into a three-tier system: Village Dairy Cooperative Societies (VDCS), District Cooperative Milk Producers' Unions, and State Cooperative Milk Federations. Under the direction of the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) and the Ministry of Cooperation, modern automated milk testing units (AMTU), bulk milk coolers (BMC), and transparent digital payment systems directly credit milk pouring farmers within 24 to 48 hours, eliminating middlemen and empowering rural women dairy farmers.

TOPIC: Fisheries Cooperatives and Blue Economy Convergence
Fisheries cooperative societies organize inland and marine fishers to collectively access modern fishing trawlers, safety gear, landing facilities, cold storage, and organized fish retail markets. Under the Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY) and national cooperative schemes, fisheries cooperatives receive financial subsidies for pond construction, recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS), biofloc technology, and working capital through Fisheries KCCs, ensuring sustainable aquatic harvest and fair price realization.

TOPIC: Cooperative Housing Societies and Flat Owners' Rights
Cooperative housing societies are formed by apartment owners or plot allottees to collectively manage shared property, security, and municipal amenities. Members pay monthly maintenance charges in proportion to carpet area or equal shares as decided in the general body meeting. Bye-laws protect individual rights against arbitrary penalties or unauthorized alterations. A housing society cannot unreasonably withhold a No Objection Certificate (NOC) for selling, leasing, or mortgaging a flat, and any unjust denial can be contested before the Cooperative Court or Registrar.

TOPIC: Member Rights, Voting Principles, and Obligations
The foundational principle of cooperative enterprise is "one member, one vote," irrespective of individual shareholding size, preventing wealthy shareholders from dominating society decisions. Active members are entitled to attend general body meetings, inspect books and registers (such as voter list and audited balance sheet), receive patronage bonus, and vote in board elections. In return, members are obligated to utilize the society's core services up to the minimum statutory requirement (failing which they risk reclassification as non-active members) and settle dues in accordance with the society's bye-laws.

TOPIC: Winding Up, Dissolution, and Role of the Liquidator
When a cooperative society becomes non-viable, fails to start operations within the stipulated period, or continuously violates its statutory mandate, the Registrar or Central Registrar can issue an order for winding up. A Liquidator is appointed to take custody of society assets, books, and claims. The Liquidator determines priority of debts, realizes outstanding dues, settles secured and unsecured creditors, and, if any surplus remains, distributes it in accordance with the statutory rules and registered bye-laws. Once the affairs are completely wound up, the Registrar issues a formal cancellation of registration, dissolving the body corporate.

TOPIC: Grievance Redressal Protocol for Primary Cooperative Members
Cooperative members seeking redress for denial of voting rights, non-issuance of passbooks, irregular loan deductions, or non-disbursement of crop insurance subsidies must first submit a written representation to the society's Secretary or Managing Committee. If unresolved within 15 days, the member may escalate the petition to the Assistant Registrar of Cooperative Societies (ARCS) or District Cooperative Officer (DCO). In multi-state societies, complaints can be filed online directly with the Cooperative Ombudsman or through the CRCS Grievance Portal. This tiered mechanism ensures expedited resolution without expensive court litigation.

TOPIC: Regular Member versus Nominal and Associate Member
Under cooperative laws and model bye-laws, a Regular (Ordinary) Member holds full equity shares, participates in the general body meeting, possesses full voting rights, is entitled to dividends, and can contest elections for the managing committee. In contrast, a Nominal or Associate Member is admitted purely for specific transactional purposes—such as taking a loan against fixed deposits, gold, or surety, or joint property ownership—without having any right to vote, attend general meetings, share in dividends, or contest elections. A nominal member cannot be held liable for the debts or liabilities of the cooperative society upon liquidation.

TOPIC: Scale of Finance and Kisan Credit Card (KCC) Calculation
The credit limit under a Kisan Credit Card (KCC) sanctioned by a PACS is computed based on the Scale of Finance (SOF) determined annually by the District Level Technical Committee (DLTC). The formula considers the cost of cultivation per acre for crops grown by the farmer, plus 10% toward post-harvest, household, and consumption requirements, plus 20% for maintenance of farm assets and crop insurance. For subsequent years (up to 5 years tenure), an annual 10% limit enhancement is provisioned. For small and marginal farmers, a simplified flexible KCC limit between Rs 10,000 to Rs 50,000 is provided without land encumbrance certificates.

TOPIC: Modified Interest Subvention Scheme (MISS) and 4 Percent Crop Loans
Under the Central Government's Modified Interest Subvention Scheme (MISS), farmers availing short-term crop loans up to Rs 3,00,000 through PACS or rural banks are eligible for interest subvention. The standard benchmark interest rate of 9% per annum is subsidized by a 1.5% interest subvention paid directly to lending institutions, setting the lending rate at 7%. Furthermore, farmers who repay their dues on or before the due date receive an additional 3% Prompt Repayment Incentive (PRI). This brings the effective annual interest rate down to just 4% per annum for prompt repayers.

TOPIC: Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) through PACS
Primary Agricultural Credit Societies act as pivotal nodal distribution points for crop insurance under Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) and Restructured Weather Based Crop Insurance Scheme (RWBCIS). When a farmer obtains a seasonal crop loan via PACS, crop insurance is debited and auto-enrolled through the National Crop Insurance Portal (NCIP) unless the farmer submits a formal opt-out form within the prescribed cutoff date. Farmers pay a nominal premium of 2% of sum insured for Kharif crops, 1.5% for Rabi crops, and 5% for commercial/horticultural crops, with the remaining actuarial premium split equally between Central and State Governments.

TOPIC: PACS Transformation into PM Kisan Samriddhi Kendras (PMKSK)
Under an initiative by the Ministry of Chemicals & Fertilizers and the Ministry of Cooperation, operational PACS dealing in fertilizer distribution have been upgraded to Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samriddhi Kendras (PMKSK). PMKSK-PACS function as integrated agricultural one-stop shops offering certified seeds, nano-urea, nano-DAP, bio-fertilizers, soil testing facilities, customized agricultural advisories, and weather forecasts. They also serve as information points for best farming practices and government schemes, eliminating black marketing and adulteration of farm inputs.

TOPIC: PACS as Fair Price Shops (FPS) under Public Distribution System
To augment recurring income and serve remote populations, the Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution authorized States to accord priority to PACS for allotment of Fair Price Shops (FPS). Operating FPS allows PACS to manage public grain distribution through electronic Point of Sale (e-PoS) devices with biometric Aadhaar authentication. This operational diversification ensures consistent commission revenue for PACS while assuring rural ration card holders timely access to subsidized foodgrains without dealer corruption.

TOPIC: PACS Establishing Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Kendras
To provide affordable generic medicines in rural hinterlands, PACS have been authorized to establish Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Kendras (PMBJK). PACS receive one-time financial assistance of up to Rs 2.50 lakh (Rs 1.50 lakh for furniture/fixtures and Rs 1.00 lakh for computer systems and refrigerators) from the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Bureau of India (PMBI). Generic medicines and surgical products are sold at 50% to 90% lower prices compared to branded medicines, providing healthcare affordability and recurring business margins for PACS.

TOPIC: PACS Operating Retail Petrol, Diesel, and LPG Distributorships
Under liberalized guidelines issued by the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas, PACS are eligible to apply for combined Category (CC) retail petroleum outlets and LPG distribution agencies. Public Sector Oil Marketing Companies (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL) grant reservations and priority allocations to financially sound PACS with suitable land parcels. This enables PACS to supply high-speed diesel directly to tractor and harvester owners, small pumpsets, and rural households, turning cooperative societies into rural commercial hubs.

TOPIC: Custom Hiring Centres (CHC) for Farm Machinery operated by PACS
Under the Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization (SMAM) and the Agricultural Infrastructure Fund (AIF), PACS are funded to establish Custom Hiring Centres (CHCs). These centres house expensive modern machinery such as heavy tractors, laser land levellers, happy seeders, combine harvesters, drone sprayers, and rotavators. Small and marginal farmers who cannot afford to buy capital-intensive machinery can hire equipment from their local PACS at nominal hourly or per-acre rates, drastically lowering the cost of cultivation.

TOPIC: Target of Two Lakh New Multipurpose PACS, Dairy, and Fisheries Cooperatives
The Union Cabinet approved a landmark plan to establish 2,00,000 new multipurpose PACS, Primary Dairy Cooperatives, and Primary Fishermen Cooperative Societies over a five-year horizon. This scheme focuses on establishing at least one viable cooperative society in every uncovered Gram Panchayat and large village across India. The initiative is executed through convergence of schemes like the Central Computerization Scheme, National Programme for Dairy Development (NPDD), PM Matsya Sampada Yojana (PMMSY), and NABARD/NDDB/NFDB technical assistance.

TOPIC: White Revolution 2.0 and Strengthening Village Dairy Cooperatives
White Revolution 2.0 is an ambitious initiative spearheaded by the Ministry of Cooperation and NDDB to expand the footprint of village dairy cooperatives. The initiative targets establishing cooperative milk societies in uncovered panchayats, linking them to district unions, providing cold-chain infrastructure, chilling plants, and cattle-feed plants. It promotes breed improvement through artificial insemination, digital milk testing, and direct benefit transfer (DBT) of milk payments, specifically incentivizing women milk producers through dedicated women dairy cooperatives.

TOPIC: National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC) and Yuva Sahakar Scheme
The National Cooperative Development Corporation (NCDC) is an apex statutory promotional financing institution under the Ministry of Cooperation. NCDC finances cooperative infrastructure, marketing, cold chains, processing mills, and hospitals. Under the 'Yuva Sahakar' scheme, NCDC provides low-interest loans and venture capital support (up to 80% project cost for special categories) to encourage youth-led cooperative startups. Other flagship NCDC programs include 'Ayushmaan Sahakar' for healthcare infrastructure and 'Nandini Sahakar' for women cooperative entrepreneurship.

TOPIC: Income Tax Concessions for Cooperative Societies: Section 115BAE
The Finance Act, 2023 introduced Section 115BAE in the Income Tax Act, 1961, providing a historic concession to new manufacturing cooperative societies. Any manufacturing cooperative society registered on or after April 1, 2023, that commences manufacturing before March 31, 2024 (and subsequent statutory extensions) is eligible for a concessional flat corporate income tax rate of 15% (plus 10% surcharge and 4% cess), bringing parity between manufacturing cooperatives and newly incorporated corporate companies.

TOPIC: Reduction of Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT) and Surcharge for Cooperatives
To establish a level playing field between corporate companies and cooperative societies, the Central Government reduced the Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT) on cooperative societies from 18.5% down to 15%. Furthermore, the surcharge on income tax for cooperative societies having total income between Rs 1 crore and Rs 10 crore was reduced from 12% to 7%. This substantial fiscal relief leaves larger surplus reserves with cooperative societies for business reinvestment and dividend distribution.

TOPIC: Enhanced Cash Transaction Limits for PACS under Section 269SS, 269T, and 269ST
Addressing severe cash liquidity difficulties faced by rural farmers, the Central Government amended the Income Tax Act rules:
1. Section 269SS and 269T: The threshold limit for accepting deposits in cash and repaying loans in cash by PACS and Primary Agricultural and Rural Development Banks (PCARDBs) to their members was raised from Rs 20,000 to Rs 2,00,000 per member.
2. Section 194N: The TDS threshold on cash withdrawals by cooperative societies without attracting tax deduction was increased from Rs 1 crore to Rs 3 crore per financial year.

TOPIC: Deductions for Cooperative Societies under Section 80P of the Income Tax Act
Section 80P of the Income Tax Act provides 100% tax deductions on profits derived by cooperative societies from specified eligible businesses. These include: (a) carrying on the business of banking or providing credit facilities to members (for primary credit societies/PACS), (b) marketing agricultural produce grown by members, (c) purchasing agricultural implements, seeds, and livestock for supply to members, (d) processing without the aid of power, and (e) cottage industries. However, under Section 80P(4), these deductions are not available to commercial cooperative banks (other than PACS and PCARDBs).

TOPIC: Surcharge Proceedings under Section 108 and 109 of the MSCS Act
If, during an audit, inquiry, inspection, or winding up of a multi-state cooperative society, it is found that any person who has taken part in the organization or management of the society, or any past or present officer or director, has misapplied, retained, or become liable or accountable for any money or property of the society, or has committed breach of trust or gross negligence, the Central Registrar may initiate Surcharge Proceedings. The Central Registrar can pass an order directing the delinquent individual to repay or restore the money with interest or contribute to the assets of the society as compensation.

TOPIC: Statutory Offences and Penalties under the MSCS (Amendment) Act 2023
The MSCS Amendment Act, 2023 instituted stringent penal provisions under Section 104 to deter fraud, manipulation, and election malpractice. Any cooperative society, director, or officer who wilfully makes a false return, furnishes false information, fails to handover records to the Cooperative Election Authority, or unlawfully withholds books from auditors or liquidators can be punished with imprisonment up to six months, or with fines extending from Rs 25,000 up to Rs 1,00,000, or both. Continued failure attracts additional daily fines.

TOPIC: Cooperative Information Officer (CIO) and Member Right to Information
To ensure operational transparency without compromising sensitive commercial information, Section 106 of the amended MSCS Act mandates every multi-state cooperative society to designate a Cooperative Information Officer (CIO). Any active member of the society can file a written application requesting access to lawful society records, such as audited balance sheets, profit and loss statements, bye-laws, election rules, and voter lists. The CIO is legally bound to provide certified copies within thirty days upon payment of prescribed copying fees.

TOPIC: Restrictions on Holding of Shares and Individual Capital Limits
To prevent concentration of power in a single individual, cooperative legislation imposes strict limits on the maximum value of shares an individual member can hold. Under Section 33 of the MSCS Act, no individual member (other than Central Government, State Governments, or apex cooperative corporations) can hold more than such portion of the total share capital of the society as is specified in the bye-laws, which generally shall not exceed one-fifth (20%) of the total subscribed share capital, or Rs 1,00,000, whichever is less.

TOPIC: Nomination, Transmission, and Transfer of Shares on Member Death
A member of a cooperative society may nominate a person in whose favour the society shall transfer their share or interest in the event of death. Upon the demise of the member, the society transfers the share or pays the equivalent value (calculated as per bye-laws) to the registered nominee within a statutory period (typically 6 to 12 months), provided the nominee qualifies for membership under the bye-laws. In the absence of a registered nomination, the value is transferred to the legal heir determined by a succession certificate or probate issued by a competent court.

TOPIC: Procedure for Expulsion of a Member from a Cooperative Society
A member may be expelled from a cooperative society only on serious grounds, such as working against the vital interests of the society, committing repeated defaults, or engaging in fraudulent acts. Expulsion requires a strict due process: (1) issuance of a written show-cause notice giving the member at least 15 to 30 days to reply, (2) consideration of the explanation by the managing committee, (3) passing a special resolution in the General Body meeting by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting, and (4) obtaining statutory approval from the Registrar. Until the Registrar sanctions the resolution, the expulsion does not take legal effect.

TOPIC: Cooperative Education, Training, and NCCT Framework
The National Council for Cooperative Training (NCCT), an autonomous society promoted by the Ministry of Cooperation, is responsible for training, educating, and capacity-building across India's cooperative ecosystem. NCCT operates the prestigious Vaikunth Mehta National Institute of Cooperative Management (VAMNICOM) at Pune, along with 5 Regional Institutes of Cooperative Management (RICMs) and 14 Institutes of Cooperative Management (ICMs). These institutions conduct professional MBA programs, executive diploma courses, and refresher trainings for PACS managers, board directors, and state cooperative department inspectors.

TOPIC: Sugar Cooperative Mills and Ethanol Blending Policy
Cooperative sugar factories in states like Maharashtra, Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, and Karnataka represent large-scale cooperative industrial enterprises. The Ministry of Cooperation resolved historical tax issues by allowing cooperative sugar mills to claim payments made to cane farmers above the Statutory Minimum Price (SMP)/Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) as legitimate business expenditure, refunding over Rs 10,000 crore in contested tax demands. Furthermore, cooperative sugar mills receive concessional capital loans from NCDC to install modern ethanol distillation units and dual-feed boilers, directly contributing to India's 20% ethanol petrol blending mandate.

TOPIC: Cooperative Tribunal: Powers, Appellate Jurisdiction, and Limitations
Most state cooperative legislations establish a specialized Judicial Cooperative Tribunal to hear appeals against orders passed by the Registrar or awards rendered by cooperative arbitrators. The Tribunal is presided over by retired District or High Court judges. Appeals must generally be filed within a strict limitation period of sixty days from the date of the order. The decisions of the Cooperative Tribunal are final on questions of fact, and can only be challenged through writ petitions under Article 226 or Article 227 before the High Court on grounds of jurisdictional error or violation of natural justice.

TOPIC: NABARD Refinance and the Short-Term Cooperative Credit Structure (STCCS)
The National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) operates as the apex refinancing bank for the three-tier Short-Term Cooperative Credit Structure (STCCS): State Cooperative Banks (StCBs) at the apex level, District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs) at the district level, and PACS at the grassroots village level. NABARD sanctions concessional Short-Term (ST) credit limits to StCBs and DCCBs, which in turn channel funds to PACS to disburse seasonal agricultural production credit (crop loans) and liquidity support to farmers.

TOPIC: DICGC Deposit Insurance for Cooperative Banks up to Rupees 5 Lakh
Deposits in all registered and licensed Primary Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs), District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs), and State Cooperative Banks (StCBs) are insured by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), a wholly-owned subsidiary of the Reserve Bank of India. In the event of a cooperative bank's liquidation, cancellation of banking licence, or amalgamation, each depositor is legally insured and guaranteed repayment of both principal and interest up to a maximum ceiling limit of Rs 5,00,000 per depositor across all accounts in that bank.

TOPIC: Electronic Negotiable Warehouse Receipts (e-NWR) and WDRA for PACS
Under the decentralised grain storage scheme, modern godowns constructed by PACS are registered with the Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority (WDRA). When farmers store their harvested foodgrains in WDRA-accredited PACS godowns, they are issued digital, tamper-proof electronic Negotiable Warehouse Receipts (e-NWRs). Farmers can pledge these e-NWRs through banking portals to instantly obtain post-harvest pledge finance of up to 75% of produce value without physical documentation, preventing distress distress sales during harvest-season market gluts.

TOPIC: 'Bharat Organics' Brand and Farmer Certification under NCOL
National Cooperative Organics Limited (NCOL) functions as an aggregator and marketing umbrella for organic farmers organized under primary cooperatives and Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs). NCOL establishes accredited soil and produce testing laboratories across the country, facilitates NPOP and PGS-India organic certification, packages produce under the 'Bharat Organics' brand, and distributes organic pulses, grains, and spices through retail chains and export channels, ensuring organic farmers receive premium farm-gate prices.

TOPIC: 'Bharat Seeds' Multiplication and Distribution Network under BBSSL
Bharatiya Beej Sahakari Samiti Limited (BBSSL) acts as the national apex cooperative for certified seed multiplication, testing, and distribution. Formed jointly by IFFCO, KRIBHCO, NAFED, NDDB, and NCDC, BBSSL contracts primary agricultural cooperative societies and progressive seed-growing farmers to cultivate foundation and certified seeds under strict quality supervision. The certified high-yield varieties (HYV) and traditional indigenous seeds are distributed under the brand 'Bharat Seeds' through the extensive PACS network at affordable rates.

TOPIC: Export Facilitation and Global Market Access via NCEL
National Cooperative Exports Limited (NCEL) was created to position India's cooperative sector in global trade. Backed by apex entities like Amul (GCMMF), IFFCO, and NAFED, NCEL procures surplus agricultural commodities, spices, sugar, rice, and dairy products directly from primary and district cooperatives. By negotiating government-to-government (G2G) export contracts and international trade deals, NCEL passes export margins directly to rural producer cooperatives, enhancing agricultural foreign exchange earnings and rural income.

TOPIC: Rochdale Principles and International Cooperative Alliance Values
The global cooperative identity is defined by the seven Rochdale Principles reaffirmed by the International Cooperative Alliance (ICA): (1) Voluntary and Open Membership without gender, social, racial, or religious discrimination; (2) Democratic Member Control with equal voting rights (one member, one vote); (3) Member Economic Participation, where capital receives limited compensation and surpluses are allocated for society development or distributed equitably; (4) Autonomy and Independence from external governmental or corporate control; (5) Education, Training, and Information for members, elected representatives, and the general public; (6) Cooperation among Cooperatives through local, national, and international networks; and (7) Concern for Community through sustainable social, economic, and environmental development policies.

TOPIC: Doctrine of Ultra Vires in Cooperative Bye-Laws
Under cooperative jurisprudence, the registered bye-laws of a cooperative society, framed within the boundaries of the governing Cooperative Societies Act, constitute the supreme internal constitutional document. Any contract entered into, resolution passed, commercial transaction executed, or loan sanctioned by the managing committee or Chief Executive Officer that goes beyond the authorized objectives and powers enumerated in the registered bye-laws is ultra vires (beyond legal power) and ab initio void. Directors who authorize ultra vires transactions incur joint and several personal liability for any financial loss suffered by the society as a result.

TOPIC: Multi-State Cooperative Societies: Branch Expansion and Approval Procedure
A Multi-State Cooperative Society desiring to open new branches, area offices, or sub-offices beyond its initially sanctioned state boundaries must follow strict regulatory steps: (1) check that its registered bye-laws explicitly provide for an area of operation covering the target states, (2) pass a formal board resolution and general body resolution for branch expansion, (3) submit a formal application to the Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS) demonstrating financial viability, non-default audit status, capital adequacy, and the requirement of the new branch, and (4) obtain statutory clearance from the CRCS. Opening branches without CRCS approval constitutes an offence punishable under Section 104 of the MSCS Act.

TOPIC: Conversion of a State Cooperative Society into a Multi-State Cooperative Society
When a cooperative society registered under a State Cooperative Societies Act wishes to expand its objects and business operations to one or more other states, it can apply for conversion into a Multi-State Cooperative Society under Section 22 of the MSCS Act. The society must convene a special general meeting, pass a conversion resolution by a two-thirds majority of members present and voting, amend its bye-laws to conform with the MSCS Act 2002 and 2023 Rules, and submit the proposal along with a 'No Objection Certificate' (NOC) or clearance from the State Registrar of Cooperative Societies to the Central Registrar. Upon satisfaction, the CRCS registers the society as a multi-state cooperative body corporate.

TOPIC: Conversion of a Multi-State Cooperative Society into a State Cooperative Society
A multi-state cooperative society whose operations become restricted to a single state or which voluntarily resolves to operate within one state boundary may convert into a State Cooperative Society under Section 23 of the MSCS Act. The conversion requires passing a special resolution by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting in a general meeting convened with due notice, giving written notice to all creditors allowing them the option to withdraw their deposits or demand debt settlement, and obtaining formal sanction from both the Central Registrar and the respective State Registrar.

TOPIC: Rights and Restrictions of Co-opted Directors under the MSCS Act
To infuse specialized professional capability into board decisions, the MSCS (Amendment) Act 2023 allows the board to co-opt up to two directors possessing recognized expertise in fields such as banking, agricultural science, management, law, finance, or cooperative administration. However, to safeguard member democracy, the law strictly stipulates that co-opted directors: (a) shall not have the right to vote in the election of the Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, or any office-bearer of the society, (b) shall not be eligible to be elected as an office-bearer, and (c) shall not be counted towards the quorum required for holding a board meeting.

TOPIC: Role and Responsibilities of Chief Executive Officer (CEO) in Cooperatives
Under Section 51 and 52 of the MSCS Act, every multi-state cooperative society must appoint a full-time Chief Executive Officer (CEO) or Managing Director who functions as the administrative head under the general superintendence and control of the board. The CEO is responsible for: (a) day-to-day administrative management, (b) operating bank accounts and managing investments as authorized by the board, (c) signing agreements, legal instruments, and statutory returns, (d) convening general body and board meetings in consultation with the Chairperson, (e) maintaining statutory registers and accounting books, and (f) assisting the board in formulating annual budgets and operational policies.

TOPIC: Powers and Statutory Functions of the Chairperson and Vice-Chairperson
The Chairperson is the elected constitutional head of the cooperative society. Core duties include presiding over all general body meetings and board of directors meetings, maintaining decorum, guiding policy deliberations, ensuring adherence to the agenda, and exercising a casting vote in the event of an equality of votes (unless bye-laws state otherwise). In the absence or incapacity of the Chairperson, the Vice-Chairperson exercises all powers and performs all functions of the Chairperson. Neither the Chairperson nor the Vice-Chairperson is permitted to interfere in the day-to-day administrative executive functions reserved for the Chief Executive Officer.

TOPIC: Preparation, Publication, and Finalization of Cooperative Voter Lists
Free and fair elections depend on a transparent voter list. Under the Cooperative Election Rules, the Chief Executive Officer must prepare a provisional list of eligible active voting members as of the qualifying date specified by the election authority. The provisional list is published on the society notice board, website, and branch offices for at least 15 to 30 days to invite claims and objections. Objections regarding wrongful inclusion or exclusion are heard and decided by the designated Returning Officer or Election Authority. Only members appearing on the finalized voter list are entitled to cast their vote or contest for board directorships.

TOPIC: Postal Ballot and Electronic Voting in Cooperative Societies
To facilitate democratic participation in large, multi-state, or geographically dispersed cooperative societies, the MSCS Rules allow general body voting and elections to be conducted via postal ballot or secure electronic voting (e-voting). Bye-laws must explicitly provision for e-voting mechanisms. The electronic voting system must provide end-to-end encryption, secret ballot integrity, audit trails, and multi-factor authentication (such as Aadhaar or mobile OTP) to prevent identity theft or duplicate voting. The Returning Officer oversees the tallying of digital and postal votes, ensuring compliance with Election Authority guidelines.

TOPIC: Special General Body Meetings: Requisition, Notice, and Legal Mandate
A Special General Meeting (SGM) of a cooperative society may be convened at any time by the board on its own motion. Furthermore, the board is legally obligated to convene an SGM within thirty to forty-five days upon receiving a written requisition signed by at least one-fifth of the total active members, or upon a requisition from the Registrar. If the board fails to convene the meeting within the statutory period, the Registrar or the requisitioning members themselves have the legal power to convene the meeting, and the expenses incurred shall be recoverable from the defaulting directors who failed to convene it.

TOPIC: Quorum Failures and Adjourned General Body Meetings
The bye-laws of a cooperative society specify the minimum quorum required to conduct a valid general body meeting (typically one-fifth of total members or a fixed number such as 100 members). If a quorum is not present within thirty minutes of the scheduled time, the meeting stands adjourned to a later hour on the same day or to a future date specified in the notice. At the adjourned meeting, the business on the original agenda may be transacted regardless of whether a quorum is present, provided that no new subject or constitutional amendment is introduced at such an adjourned meeting.

TOPIC: Minutes of General Body and Board Meetings: Statutory Maintenance and Evidentiary Value
Every cooperative society must record true and accurate minutes of all proceedings of general body meetings and board meetings in books kept exclusively for that purpose. Minutes must record names of members or directors present, resolutions moved, details of voting, and dissent noted. Minutes must be signed by the Chairperson of the meeting within thirty days. Under cooperative evidence law, properly signed minutes recorded in conformity with statutory rules serve as prima facie evidence of the proceedings, and decisions recorded therein are presumed to have been legally taken unless proven otherwise.

TOPIC: Resignation of Directors and Filling of Casual Vacancies
A director may resign from the board by tendering written notice to the Chairperson or Chief Executive Officer. The resignation takes effect on the date specified in the notice or upon formal acceptance by the board. When a casual vacancy arises on the board due to death, resignation, disqualification, or removal, the remaining directors may fill the vacancy by co-option from the eligible membership category, provided the remaining tenure does not exceed half of the original term. If the remaining tenure exceeds half of the board term, a fresh election must be notified through the Cooperative Election Authority.

TOPIC: Motion of No-Confidence against Office-Bearers in Cooperative Societies
Members of the managing committee or the general body can move a motion of no-confidence against the Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson, or any office-bearer on grounds of incompetence, dereliction of duty, or loss of majority support. The motion must be signed by a prescribed minimum proportion of directors or members (usually one-third), and submitted to the Registrar or designated administrative authority. A special meeting is convened within thirty days, presided over by a government officer. If the motion is carried by a requisite majority (typically two-thirds of members present and voting), the office-bearer stands removed immediately.

TOPIC: Attachment of Property Before Award in Cooperative Disputes
Under Section 95 of the MSCS Act (and corresponding State Cooperative Acts), if the Arbitrator or Registrar is satisfied that a party to a dispute, with intent to defeat, delay, or obstruct the execution of any award or order that may be passed, is about to dispose of or remove their property from the local jurisdiction, the Arbitrator or Registrar may issue an order for conditional attachment of the property before the final award is made. Such attachment carries the same legal effect as an attachment ordered by a competent Civil Court under Order XXXVIII of the Code of Civil Procedure.

TOPIC: Execution of Cooperative Arbitral Awards and Decrees
An arbitral award, decision, or order passed under Section 84 of the MSCS Act is legally final and binding. If the judgment-debtor fails to satisfy the award, the award-holder can enforce it through two recognized statutory channels: (1) applying to the Registrar to execute the award through the Recovery Officer / Sale Officer via attachment and sale of the debtor movable or immovable properties, or (2) filing an execution application before the competent Civil Court having territorial jurisdiction, where the award is executed in the exact manner as a civil court decree under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908.

TOPIC: Appointment and Legal Powers of an Administrator upon Board Supersession
When a managing committee is superseded by the Registrar for persistent default, negligence, or corruption, an Administrator or a Board of Administrators is appointed under Section 123 of the MSCS Act to assume charge of the society. The Administrator steps into the shoes of the board and exercises all administrative, financial, and operational powers of the committee. The Administrator primary mandate is to streamline financial records, recover outstanding dues, rectify operational defects, and conduct fresh, fair elections to hand back management to a democratically elected board within the maximum statutory timeframe (usually six months to one year).

TOPIC: Custody and Handover of Records during Leadership Transition
When the term of a managing committee expires, or a committee is superseded, or an office-bearer resigns, all statutory registers, accounting ledgers, cash balances, digital keys, and property titles must be handed over immediately to the incoming board, administrator, or authorized officer. Section 107 of the MSCS Act empowers the Registrar or Magistrate to issue a search warrant authorizing police assistance to enter premises, break open locks, and seize society records from any recalcitrant past officer who unlawfully withholds them.

TOPIC: Annual Return Filing Requirements on the CRCS Digital Portal
Every multi-state cooperative society must file its statutory Annual Returns with the Central Registrar of Cooperative Societies (CRCS) within six months of the close of each financial year. The annual return must be accompanied by: (a) audited balance sheet and profit & loss account, (b) annual report approved by the general body, (c) report of the auditor and compliance notes on defects, (d) list of amendments to bye-laws approved during the year, (e) declaration of dividend and reserve fund transfers, and (f) details of loans granted to directors or their relatives. Failure to file returns attracts statutory fines and operational restrictions.

TOPIC: Penalties for Accepting Deposits from Non-Members
Cooperative credit societies (such as thrift and credit societies or primary non-banking cooperatives) are legally prohibited from soliciting or accepting public deposits from non-members. Accepting deposits from the general public without an RBI banking licence violates both the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 and the Banning of Unregulated Deposit Schemes (BUDS) Act, 2019. Any cooperative found soliciting public funds under the guise of cooperative shares or fixed deposits faces immediate regulatory freezing of bank accounts, criminal prosecution of promoters, attachment of assets, and winding-up proceedings.

TOPIC: Restrictions on Loans to Directors and Their Relatives
To prevent self-dealing, conflict of interest, and crony lending, Section 67 of the MSCS Act and RBI guidelines impose strict curbs on loans to directors. A multi-state cooperative society cannot sanction loans, advances, or credit guarantees to any director, the Chief Executive Officer, or their immediate family members (spouses, parents, children, siblings) without prior disclosure, special board evaluation, and conformity with RBI/CRCS ceiling limits. In cooperative banks, sanctioning loans to directors or their interested firms is completely prohibited under Section 20 of the Banking Regulation Act.

TOPIC: Investment of Funds by Cooperative Societies outside Core Business
A cooperative society cannot invest its funds in speculative markets, equity shares of commercial companies, or unapproved schemes. Under Section 63 of the MSCS Act, a society may invest or deposit its funds only in: (a) State Cooperative Banks or District Central Cooperative Banks, (b) securities of the Central or State Government, (c) postal savings banks, (d) shares of other cooperative societies with similar objectives, or (e) any scheduled commercial bank approved by the Registrar. Any diversion of funds into unauthorized mutual funds, chit funds, or real estate schemes is treated as breach of trust.

TOPIC: Cooperative Bad Debt and Provisioning Norms
Cooperative societies engaged in lending must follow statutory asset classification and provisioning guidelines to maintain solvency. Loans are classified into Standard, Sub-Standard, Doubtful, and Loss assets based on overdue duration. Societies must maintain minimum mandatory provisions out of their operating profits: 10% on sub-standard assets, 20% to 100% on doubtful assets depending on collateral security coverage, and 100% on loss assets. Bad debts can only be formally written off from the Bad Debt Reserve with the prior recommendation of the statutory auditor and formal approval of the general body.

TOPIC: Write-Off of Non-Performing Assets in Cooperative Banks
In Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) and State Cooperative Banks, non-performing assets (NPAs) that have defied recovery despite legal proceedings, SARFAESI actions, or arbitration may be evaluated for technical or prudential write-off. Write-off does not mean loan waiver for the borrower; the bank retains full legal rights to pursue recovery and attach properties. Every write-off proposal must be scrutinized by an independent Audit Committee of the Board, approved by the Board of Directors, and documented in conformity with the RBI Master Directions on Stressed Assets.

TOPIC: Prompt Corrective Action Framework for Urban Cooperative Banks
The Reserve Bank of India introduced a comprehensive Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework for Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) to initiate structured, timely remedial interventions when financial indicators deteriorate. The PCA framework is triggered when a UCB breaches predetermined threshold triggers related to: (a) Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets Ratio (CRAR) falling below statutory minimums, (b) Net Non-Performing Assets (Net NPAs) exceeding 6% to 12%, or (c) incurrence of net losses for two consecutive years. Sanctions include restrictions on dividend distribution, branch expansion, CEO salary hikes, and caps on lending to specific sectors.

TOPIC: Board of Management Requirement in Urban Cooperative Banks
Following RBI guidelines, every Urban Cooperative Bank (UCB) having deposits of Rs 100 crore or more must constitute an expert Board of Management (BoM) in addition to the democratically elected Board of Directors (BoD). While the BoD handles overall governance and policy direction, the BoM consists of technocrats and banking professionals specializing in credit risk, accountancy, information technology, and treasury operations. The BoM oversees credit appraisal, loan sanctions, risk mitigation, and regulatory compliance, bridging the gap between democratic control and modern banking expertise.

TOPIC: Cooperative Banks Lending Limits to Priority Sector Lending
Under RBI Master Directions, Primary Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) must achieve a Priority Sector Lending (PSL) target of 75% of their Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) or Credit Equivalent Amount of Off-Balance Sheet Exposure (CEOBE). Sub-targets include mandatory allocations toward micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs), agriculture, education, affordable housing, and weaker sections. Adhering to PSL norms ensures that cooperative credit remains focused on supporting underserved economic segments and grassroots rural-urban entrepreneurs.

TOPIC: Housing Cooperatives: Transfer Charges Ceiling and Legal Restrictions
Cooperative housing society bye-laws in major states (such as Model Bye-law 38 in Maharashtra) prohibit societies from demanding exorbitant premium payments or donations when a member sells or transfers their flat. The statutory maximum transfer fee that a housing society can lawfully charge is capped (typically at Rs 25,000). Demanding voluntary donations, development funds, or percentage-based transaction cuts as a precondition for issuing a No Objection Certificate (NOC) or share transfer is illegal, and flat owners can file a complaint before the Deputy Registrar or Cooperative Court for refund and punitive damages.

TOPIC: Non-Occupancy Charges in Housing Cooperatives
When a flat owner in a cooperative housing society leases, sub-lets, or licenses their flat to a tenant or third party, the society is entitled to levy a Non-Occupancy Charge. However, state cooperative circulars and standard bye-laws mandate that non-occupancy charges cannot exceed 10% of the standard monthly service/maintenance charges (excluding statutory local municipal taxes). Furthermore, if the flat is occupied by immediate family members (parents, spouse, children, siblings), the society is strictly prohibited from levying any non-occupancy charges.

TOPIC: Parking Space Allocation and Byelaws in Cooperative Housing Societies
Parking spaces (whether stilt parking, open parking, or basement slots) in a cooperative housing society form part of the common amenities and cannot be sold, rented, or commercialized by the builder or managing committee. The society general body has the sole authority to allot parking slots to registered members on a first-come, first-served or equitable rotational basis. Priority must be given to members owning registered vehicles. A member who does not own a vehicle cannot hold a parking slot or sub-let parking to non-residents.

TOPIC: Tenant Verification and Rights of Sub-Lessees in Housing Cooperatives
A member has the legal right to sub-let or rent their flat, subject to providing written intimation to the cooperative housing society along with a copy of the registered Leave and License agreement and local Police Tenant Verification acknowledgement. The managing committee cannot arbitrarily reject tenants based on religion, dietary preferences, marital status, or nationality. While sub-lessees enjoy full peaceful access to common utilities, water, lifts, and security, they do not possess voting rights or the right to attend society general meetings.

TOPIC: Deemed Conveyance Procedure for Cooperative Housing Societies
In many instances, property builders and developers fail to execute legal conveyance deeds transferring land ownership and building title to the cooperative housing society within the statutory deadline (usually four months from society registration). To protect flat owners, state legislations provide the 'Deemed Conveyance' mechanism. The society applies to the Competent Authority (District Deputy Registrar), submitting building plans, completion certificates, and registration records. After issuing notice to the defaulting promoter, the Authority passes an order granting unilateral deemed conveyance, establishing the society absolute legal ownership of the land.

TOPIC: Formation of Consumer Cooperative Societies and Kendriya Bhandar Model
Consumer cooperative societies are established by consumers to eliminate middlemen in retail distribution, stabilize retail prices, and supply unadulterated consumer goods, groceries, and household items at fair rates. Operating on wholesale procurement and low overhead margins, apex models like Kendriya Bhandar procure directly from manufacturers, farmers, and government grain reserves. Members receive rebates or dividends proportional to the value of their annual purchases (patronage dividends), promoting consumer welfare and shielding families from retail inflation.

TOPIC: Labour Contract and Construction Cooperative Societies
Labour contract cooperative societies are formed by manual labourers, skilled artisans, masons, and construction workers to free themselves from exploitative labor contractors and middlemen. State governments grant special preferences to labor cooperatives, including reservations in public civil construction tenders, exemption from earnest money deposits (EMD), and price preferences up to 10% in government public works contracts. The society distributes contract earnings directly to member workers based on labor hours contributed, with statutory contributions to EPF and accident insurance.

TOPIC: Handloom and Weavers Cooperative Societies and Apex Federations
Weavers cooperative societies organize traditional handloom and powerloom artisans into collective production units. The societies procure raw yarn, dyes, and modern design graphs in bulk, distribute them to weaver members, and collect finished fabrics. Apex handloom federations (such as Co-optex, Boyanika, and Weaver Guilds) market these hand-woven fabrics through retail showroom networks and national exhibitions. Government schemes provide working capital subsidies, interest subvention, yarn supply subsidies, and health insurance to handloom cooperative members.

TOPIC: Tribal and Forest Produce Cooperative Societies and TRIFED Convergence
Tribal cooperative marketing societies (such as Large-sized Adivasi Multi-Purpose Societies - LAMPS) operate in scheduled tribal belts to protect indigenous communities from exploitation by money lenders and timber syndicates. Linked with the Tribal Cooperative Marketing Development Federation of India (TRIFED), these cooperatives purchase Minor Forest Produce (MFP)—including wild honey, mahua flowers, tendu leaves, lac, and medicinal herbs—at guaranteed Minimum Support Price (MSP for MFP). Value addition, processing, and national marketing are carried out through the 'Van Dhan' tribal startup cluster network.

TOPIC: Self-Help Groups Linkage with Primary Agricultural Credit Societies
Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS) serve as financial intermediaries linking women Self-Help Groups (SHGs) under the Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana - National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM). PACS open group savings accounts, disburse collateral-free revolving credit and enterprise loans to SHGs, and enable rural women to undertake micro-enterprises such as dairy processing, mushroom cultivation, poultry, and local crafts. This financial linkage empowers rural women, improves recovery rates, and broadens the grassroots membership base of primary cooperatives.

TOPIC: Joint Liability Groups for Tenant and Landless Farmers via PACS
Tenant farmers, sharecroppers, and landless agricultural laborers often lack documented land titles, making them ineligible for standard individual bank credit. To bridge this gap, PACS promote Joint Liability Groups (JLGs)—informal groups of 4 to 10 small, marginal, or tenant farmers who agree to function as mutual guarantors for agricultural loans. PACS extend short-term production credit to JLGs for seasonal crop cultivation based on mutual peer pressure and group trust, ensuring that landless agricultural producers gain access to formal credit.

TOPIC: Agricultural Infrastructure Fund Financing for PACS Projects
Under the Central Sector Agricultural Infrastructure Fund (AIF), PACS are recognized as primary eligible beneficiaries for setting up post-harvest agricultural management infrastructure. The scheme offers an attractive 3% per annum interest subvention on bank loans up to Rs 2 crore for a maximum tenure of seven years, accompanied by credit guarantee coverage under the CGTMSE framework. PACS leverage AIF funding to construct multi-commodity godowns, sorting and grading sheds, primary processing mills, and cold storage units at the village level.

TOPIC: Cold Storage and Ripening Chambers Setup by Cooperatives
To prevent post-harvest perishability losses of fruits, vegetables, and flowers, cooperative societies can access specialized capital subsidies through the National Horticulture Board (NHB) and NCDC to install cold storage and controlled atmosphere (CA) ripening chambers. By storing onions, potatoes, apples, and bananas in cooperative cold stores, farmers can stagger sales throughout the off-season, capturing peak market pricing and earning higher farm-gate realizations.

TOPIC: PACS as Drone Service Providers for Precision Agriculture
Under the modern mechanization roadmap, the Ministry of Cooperation and the Ministry of Agriculture have integrated PACS into the 'Kisan Drone' ecosystem. PACS can acquire agricultural drones with government subsidies up to 100% (for select primary training centres) or 75% for cooperative hiring hubs. Drones operated by trained PACS rural youth provide cost-effective aerial spraying of liquid fertilizers (like Nano-Urea) and pesticides, dramatically saving water, reducing input costs, and shielding farmers from hazardous chemical exposure.

TOPIC: Kisan Drones and Fertilizer Spraying Subsidies through Cooperatives
Primary cooperatives receive financial assistance and logistical support from apex fertilizer cooperatives (like IFFCO and KRIBHCO) to provide custom drone spraying services. Farmers booking drone sessions through their local PACS pay nominal per-acre charges for rapid, uniform nutrient application. In addition, rural women associated with cooperative self-help groups are trained as drone pilots under the 'Drone Didi' initiative, opening new avenues of high-tech rural employment and cooperative entrepreneurship.

TOPIC: Rural Piped Drinking Water Supply Operations by PACS
Under the 'Har Ghar Jal' mission (Jal Jeevan Mission), State Governments and the Ministry of Cooperation have issued frameworks authorizing functional PACS to undertake operation and maintenance (O&M) contracts for rural piped water supply schemes. PACS manage water pumping stations, pipeline repairs, chlorine dosing, and periodic water quality testing, while collecting water service tariffs from village households. This initiative provides PACS with steady monthly service revenue while ensuring uninterrupted safe drinking water supply to rural communities.

TOPIC: Model Byelaws on Environmental Sustainability and Green Energy
The Model Bye-Laws for PACS incorporate explicit provisions mandating environmental sustainability and adoption of renewable green energy. Cooperatives are empowered to generate, distribute, and utilize solar power, manage decentralized solid and liquid waste, promote organic and natural farming, and install biogas plants. Embracing green energy lowers the operating overhead of PACS offices and cold storages, creating eco-friendly village institutions aligned with national climate commitments.

TOPIC: Solar Rooftop and PM-KUSUM Component-A Integration with PACS
Under Component-A of the PM-KUSUM scheme, individual farmers, cooperatives, and PACS can install grid-connected solar power plants ranging from 500 kW to 2 MW on fallow or uncultivable lands located within a 5 km radius of rural distribution sub-stations. The electricity generated is purchased by local distribution companies (DISCOMs) at feed-in tariffs determined by State Electricity Regulatory Commissions (SERCs). PACS can earn stable, risk-free long-term power purchase revenue for 25 years, while supplying daytime green power for irrigation pumpsets.

TOPIC: Agro-Processing and Value Addition Infrastructure at PACS Level
Under the 'Pradhan Mantri Formalisation of Micro food processing Enterprises' (PMFME) scheme and cooperative convergence plans, PACS receive credit-linked capital subsidies to establish village-level agro-processing units. Typical installations include mini-rice mills, flour and dal mills, mustard and groundnut oil expellers, and spice grinding facilities. Processing harvested produce at the village boundary eliminates transportation costs, creates local value addition, and generates branded regional food products marketed through cooperative networks.

TOPIC: Bio-fertilizers, Vermicompost, and Waste-to-Wealth Units in Village Cooperatives
To restore soil health and reduce reliance on synthetic chemical fertilizers, PACS and dairy cooperatives are actively promoting bio-fertilizers, vermicompost pits, and cow dung-based biogas units (under the GOBARdhan scheme). Village dairy cooperatives collect cattle dung, process it in bio-gas digestors, and produce compressed biogas (CBG) and nutrient-rich Fermented Organic Manure (FOM). FOM is bagged and sold through PACS to farmers as organic compost, generating sustainable waste-to-wealth circular economy revenues for rural households.

TOPIC: National Cooperation Policy: Long-Term Vision for Sahakar se Samriddhi
The National Cooperation Policy drafted by a high-level national committee formulates a strategic blueprint for realizing the Prime Minister vision of 'Sahakar se Samriddhi' (Prosperity through Cooperation). The policy focuses on: (1) creating an enabling legal and regulatory framework across all states, (2) establishing vibrant multipurpose cooperatives in every panchayat, (3) promoting technological modernization, digital governance, and transparency, (4) expanding access to low-cost institutional credit and venture capital, (5) establishing a National Cooperative University for specialized professional education, and (6) integrating Indian cooperatives into national and international supply chains.

TOPIC: Legal Hierarchy Governing Farmer Cooperatives - State Acts, MSCS Act, and Model Bye-Laws
Farmer cooperatives in India operate within a layered legal hierarchy. A primary agricultural credit society confined to one state is governed by that state's Cooperative Societies Act and Rules, while societies with membership or business spanning more than one state fall under the Multi-State Cooperative Societies Act. Overlaid on both is the Model Bye-Laws framework issued by the Ministry of Cooperation, which states are encouraged to adopt so that farmer-members across the country enjoy broadly similar rights on voting, audit, loaning limits, and grievance redressal, even though the underlying statute differs from state to state.

TOPIC: Farmers' Rights under the Right to Fair Compensation and Land Acquisition Laws vis-a-vis Cooperative Land
When land held or leased by a cooperative society (such as a processing unit or godown site) is acquired for a public purpose, affected farmer-members are entitled to compensation, rehabilitation, and resettlement benefits under the applicable land acquisition law, in addition to any compensation payable to the society itself. The managing committee is legally required to hold a special general body meeting to inform members of acquisition proceedings and to ensure that individual farmer claims are not subsumed into the society's corporate claim without member consent.

TOPIC: Statutory Protection against Distress Sale and Cooperative Marketing Support Price
Several state Agricultural Produce Marketing laws, read with cooperative marketing society bye-laws, empower marketing and PACS-linked societies to procure farm produce at a support price during glut conditions, protecting farmers from distress sales to private traders. Where the market price falls below the declared Minimum Support Price (MSP), procurement is undertaken through designated cooperative agencies such as NAFED or state civil supplies corporations, with cooperatives acting as local aggregation and payment-disbursal points for farmer-sellers.

TOPIC: Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN) Disbursement through Cooperative Banking Channels
Under PM-KISAN, eligible small and marginal farmer families receive direct income support in three equal installments annually, credited directly to their bank accounts, which may be held with cooperative banks or PACS operating as banking correspondents. PACS assist farmers with e-KYC, land-record seeding, and correction of beneficiary details on the PM-KISAN portal, reducing the need for farmers to travel to distant bank branches or government offices to resolve payment failures.

TOPIC: Modified Interest Subvention Scheme and Prompt Repayment Incentive for Short-Term Crop Loans
Farmers availing short-term crop loans up to a specified ceiling (commonly up to Rs 3 lakh) through the Kisan Credit Card route via PACS and cooperative banks receive an interest subvention of 1.5 percent per annum from the government, effectively reducing the lending rate to around 7 percent. Farmers who repay their loans promptly and on time receive an additional prompt repayment incentive of around 3 percent, bringing the effective interest rate down further to around 4 percent, which is intended to discourage willful default and encourage disciplined repayment behavior.

TOPIC: Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana - Claim Assessment and Settlement Process
Under PMFBY, crop yield losses are assessed through Crop Cutting Experiments (CCEs) conducted at the notified insurance unit level (village or gram panchayat), and losses from localized calamities such as hailstorm, landslide, or inundation are assessed individually on an on-the-spot basis. Once yield or loss data is finalized and uploaded to the National Crop Insurance Portal, the empanelled insurance company is required to settle and credit claim amounts directly into the insured farmer's bank account within a prescribed timeframe, with PACS assisting farmers in filing loss intimation and tracking claim status.

TOPIC: Weather-Based Crop Insurance Scheme and Restructured Weather-Based Crop Insurance Scheme
As an alternative or supplement to yield-based insurance, the Weather-Based Crop Insurance Scheme (WBCIS) and its restructured version use data from automatic weather stations to trigger payouts when rainfall, temperature, humidity, or wind speed deviate beyond pre-defined thresholds for a notified crop and area. Because payouts are based on weather-station readings rather than field-level yield surveys, claim settlement is generally faster, and PACS and cooperative banks that disburse crop loans facilitate farmer enrolment and premium deduction at the time of loan sanction.

TOPIC: Post-Harvest and Standalone Crop Insurance Cover for Non-Loanee Farmers
While loanee farmers availing crop loans are compulsorily covered under notified crop insurance schemes, non-loanee farmers, including tenant farmers and sharecroppers without formal land documents, can also voluntarily enroll for crop insurance cover through the PACS, Common Service Centres, or insurance company portals within the notified enrolment window. PACS field staff assist such farmers in completing land-possession self-declarations and premium payment so that vulnerable cultivators without a cooperative loan are not excluded from crop insurance protection.

TOPIC: Restructuring, Rescheduling, and Moratorium on Cooperative Crop Loans during Natural Calamities
When a state government declares a natural calamity (drought, flood, cyclone) affecting a notified area, cooperative banks and PACS are directed by the Registrar and NABARD to restructure or reschedule affected farmers' short-term crop loans into medium-term loans, extend a repayment moratorium, and, in eligible cases, waive penal interest for the affected period. Farmers must generally apply through their PACS along with revenue department loss-certification documents to avail such relief.

TOPIC: NABARD-Sponsored Financial Literacy Centres and Farmer Financial Awareness Camps
NABARD, in coordination with cooperative banks and district central cooperative banks, supports Financial Literacy Centres (FLCs) and periodic Financial Literacy and Credit Counselling camps in rural areas. These camps educate farmers and rural stakeholders on responsible borrowing, the difference between formal and informal credit, digital payment safety, crop insurance benefits, pension and social security schemes, and the grievance channels available if they face harassment from unauthorized money lenders or loan recovery agents.

TOPIC: Financial Literacy and Digital Payment Training through PACS Common Service Centres
PACS functioning as Common Service Centres conduct financial literacy sessions to familiarize farmer-members with operating savings accounts, using ATM and RuPay Kisan Credit Cards, making Aadhaar-enabled payments, and safely checking loan and dividend balances through mobile applications. Special attention is given to training women self-help group members and elderly farmers who may otherwise be vulnerable to digital payment fraud or dependence on intermediaries for basic banking transactions.

TOPIC: Kisan Call Centre and Farmer Grievance Helpline
The Kisan Call Centre, accessible through a toll-free national helpline, allows farmers to raise queries or complaints regarding crop advisory, input availability, cooperative loan disbursal delays, and crop insurance claim status in their local language. Complaints that cannot be resolved at the call centre level are escalated to the concerned state agriculture department, cooperative bank, or insurance company, and farmers are given a complaint reference number to track resolution.

TOPIC: Grievance Redressal for Delayed or Rejected Crop Insurance Claims
A farmer whose crop insurance claim is delayed, under-assessed, or rejected may first raise a written grievance with the PACS or bank branch through which the loan and insurance premium were processed, and simultaneously log the complaint on the National Crop Insurance Portal or with the district-level Grievance Redressal Committee chaired by the District Collector. If unresolved, the matter can be escalated to the State Level Coordination Committee on Crop Insurance, and, in cases involving deficiency of service, to the consumer disputes redressal forum.

TOPIC: District Consumer Grievance Redressal Forums for Rural and Cooperative Banking Complaints
Rural and farmer stakeholders facing deficiency of service from a cooperative bank, such as wrongful loan account debits, mis-selling of insurance products, or unjustified account freezing, may approach the Banking Ombudsman scheme (for scheduled cooperative banks) or the district Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission. Complaints must generally first be raised in writing with the bank's internal grievance redressal officer, and only after a lapse of thirty days without resolution, or if unsatisfied with the response, can the matter be escalated externally.

TOPIC: Farmer Producer Organisation Promotion Scheme and Cooperative-FPO Convergence
Under the Central Sector Scheme for Formation and Promotion of 10,000 Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs), PACS and other primary cooperatives are encouraged to convert into or promote linked FPOs to access equity grants, credit guarantee cover, and matching equity grants for aggregation of inputs, machinery, and produce marketing. This convergence allows farmer-members to benefit from both the traditional credit and input-supply functions of PACS and the collective bargaining and market-linkage functions of FPOs under a single institutional umbrella.

TOPIC: Soil Health Card and Village-Level Input Advisory Services through PACS
PACS and dairy cooperatives, working with the state agriculture department, help disseminate Soil Health Cards to farmer-members, showing nutrient status and fertilizer recommendations for their specific land parcels. Trained PACS staff and cooperative extension volunteers use this data to advise farmers on balanced fertilizer use, reducing overuse of urea and improving both soil health and input cost efficiency at the village level.

TOPIC: e-NAM Market Linkage and Price Discovery for Cooperative Farmer-Members
Select PACS and marketing cooperatives are integrated as procurement or collection points under the National Agriculture Market (e-NAM) platform, enabling farmer-members to access real-time price information from multiple mandis before deciding where to sell their produce. This integration reduces information asymmetry, helps farmers avoid distress sales at the nearest local mandi alone, and allows cooperatives to aggregate produce for sale to buyers in other regions offering better prices.

TOPIC: Cooperative Extension Services and Krishi Vigyan Kendra Linkage for Farmer Training
PACS and district cooperative federations coordinate with Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) and state agricultural universities to organize farmer training on improved seed varieties, integrated pest management, water-efficient irrigation techniques, and post-harvest handling. These extension linkages are often bundled with cooperative input-supply services, so that farmers purchasing seeds or fertilizers through PACS also receive technical guidance on their correct application.
